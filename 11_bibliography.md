# Bibliography

*Sources used to assemble this persona dossier, plus further-reading suggestions. Grouped by topic. Not exhaustive; designed as a starting point for the downstream agent or for anyone validating the persona against primary sources.*

---

## 1. Foundational Cypherpunk Texts

- Eric Hughes, *A Cypherpunk's Manifesto* (1993). The canonical short text. Original: `https://www.activism.net/cypherpunk/manifesto.html`. The "Cypherpunks write code" line and the "privacy is the power to selectively reveal oneself" line both come from here.
- Tim May, *The Crypto Anarchist Manifesto* (1988). The more radical sibling text.
- Tim May, *The Cyphernomicon* (1994). Sprawling FAQ-style document; useful as a historical artifact of the early mailing list's worldview.
- Phil Zimmermann, *Why I Wrote PGP* (1991, revised 1999). Plain-language case for cryptography as a civic act.
- The Cypherpunks mailing list archives. Of historical interest; the cohort's tonal register descends from this list.

## 2. Cryptography and Security Engineering (Foundational Textbooks)

- Bruce Schneier, *Applied Cryptography* (1996, second edition). Dated in places (use *Cryptography Engineering*, with Ferguson and Kohno, for current best practice). Cultural touchstone.
- Bruce Schneier, Niels Ferguson, Tadayoshi Kohno, *Cryptography Engineering* (2010). The more current Schneier textbook.
- Ross Anderson, *Security Engineering*, 3rd ed. (2020). Free online. Widely cited as the most influential security textbook of the past three decades. Anderson died in 2024; the cohort has been re-reading him.
- David Wong, *Real-World Cryptography* (2021). The current generation's working reference for applied cryptography.
- Boneh and Shoup, *A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography* (work in progress, freely available). For the Protocol Cypherpunk subtype.
- Jean-Philippe Aumasson, *Serious Cryptography* (2017). Accessible modern introduction.

## 3. Key Protocols and Designs (Primary Documents)

- Roger Dingledine, Nick Mathewson, Paul Syverson, *Tor: The Second-Generation Onion Router* (USENIX Security 2004). The Tor design paper. Required reading for anyone evaluating anonymity systems.
- The Signal Protocol documentation (Open Whisper Systems / Signal Foundation). The X3DH and Double Ratchet papers.
- Trevor Perrin, *The Noise Protocol Framework*. A foundation for many modern secure-transport protocols.
- The Bitcoin Whitepaper (Nakamoto, 2008). Treated as a model of compressed technical writing.
- Adam Back, *Hashcash* (2002). Cited in the Bitcoin whitepaper.
- David Chaum, *Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments* (1983). The cryptographic ancestor of digital cash.
- The Monero documentation, particularly the *Zero to Monero* book (kovri.org / official Monero). For the Crypto-Positive subtype.
- Daniel J. Bernstein et al., the Curve25519, ChaCha20, and Poly1305 papers. The cohort's working primitive set.
- The IETF RFCs for TLS 1.3, HTTPS, the Noise Protocol, X25519, Ed25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305, Argon2, the Messaging Layer Security (MLS) protocol (RFC 9420). The cohort treats well-engineered IETF processes as a positive signal.

## 4. Local-First Software

- Martin Kleppmann, Adam Wiggins, Peter van Hardenberg, Mark McGranaghan (Ink & Switch), *Local-first software: you own your data, in spite of the cloud* (2019). The seven-ideals essay. `https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/`
- Martin Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* (2017). Foundational systems-design book; widely respected in the cohort.
- The Automerge documentation and papers. Reference CRDT implementation.
- Geoffrey Litt and other Ink & Switch essays. The ongoing research output.
- The CRDT research literature (Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero, Zawirski, *A comprehensive study of Convergent and Commutative Replicated Data Types*).

## 5. The Centralization vs Decentralization Debate

- Moxie Marlinspike, *The Ecosystem Is Moving* (talk, 36C3 Chaos Communication Congress, 2019). The most-cited "case for centralization in secure messaging." Worth reading alongside the rebuttals.
- Various rebuttals: *Dispelling Decentralization Doubts* (Thoughts on Security, 2020); *Re: The Ecosystem is Moving* (vanitasvitae); *The Ecosystem is Moving Away from Monoliths* (Freedombone Blog). These responses show the cohort's working disagreement.
- David Rosenthal, *Moxie Marlinspike On Decentralization* (DSHR's blog, 2020). A long, careful summary and partial defense of Marlinspike's view from a senior practitioner.
- Vitalik Buterin, *Credible Neutrality As a Guiding Principle* (2020). The "credible neutrality" framing now widely used in this cohort.

## 6. Web3 Skepticism and Crypto Critique

- Molly White, *Web3 is Going Just Great* (running site). The definitive ongoing chronicle of crypto failures.
- Stephen Diehl, various essays. Long-running, technically grounded crypto-skeptic position.
- David Rosenthal, *It Isn't About The Technology*, *Special Report on Decentralizing the Internet*, and related blog posts.
- Cory Doctorow, the corpus on "enshittification" and "adversarial interoperability." Both terms are now part of the cohort's vocabulary.
- *Bitcoin and the Rise of the Cypherpunks* (CoinDesk, 2016) and the longer Open Bitcoin Privacy Project archives. For the cypherpunk wing's complex relationship with Bitcoin.

## 7. Threat Modeling

- *Threat Modeling: The First Step on Your Privacy Journey* (Privacy Guides). A practical introduction widely used in privacy-activist contexts.
- The OWASP Threat Modeling resources.
- *CT-001: Threat modeling demystified* (Monero Observer, Cypherpunk Transmission series). Threat modeling as cypherpunk practice.
- Adam Shostack, *Threat Modeling: Designing for Security* (2014). Industry standard reference.
- NIST guidance on threat models for differential privacy and related systems.
- The LINDDUN privacy threat modeling methodology (KU Leuven).

## 8. Open-Source Contribution Research

- *The Signals that Potential Contributors Look for When Choosing Open-source Projects* (Qiu et al.). Empirical research on what makes contributors join.
- *Trust Signals in Open Source Projects* (HackerNoon, summarizing the above and adjacent work).
- *Trust in open source communities* (Duck Alignment Academy, 2026). On evolving trust signals in the AI-PR era.
- *Welcome to the Eternal September of open source* (GitHub Blog, 2026). On the volume problem in current OSS contribution.
- *Decomposing and Measuring Trust in Open-Source Software Supply Chains* (Boughton et al., ICSE 2024).
- *Attracting and Retaining OSS Contributors with a Maintainer Dashboard* (Guizani et al., 2022).

## 9. Privacy and Anonymity Project Communities

- The Tor Project documentation, blog, and design papers.
- The Signal Foundation blog. (Moxie Marlinspike's pieces, Meredith Whittaker's posts.)
- The Privacy Guides project (`privacyguides.org`). High-trust recommendation hub used across the cohort.
- The EFF's *Surveillance Self-Defense* guide.
- Tactical Tech publications, especially on activist threat models.
- Access Now reporting on internet shutdowns and digital-rights cases.
- Freedom of the Press Foundation publications.
- The SecureDrop documentation.
- The GrapheneOS documentation and threat model.

## 10. Self-Hosting and Sovereign Computing

- The `awesome-selfhosted` GitHub repository. The reference index for the Sovereign Computing subtype.
- `r/selfhosted` (Reddit). The community's largest single forum.
- The Self-Hosted Podcast (Jupiter Broadcasting).
- The `selfh.st` newsletter.
- The Mike Royal *Self-Hosting Guide*. Comprehensive practical reference.
- The Yunohost, FreedomBox, and related distributions, each with their own documentation.

## 11. Decentralized Identity, Federation, and P2P

- The ActivityPub specification (W3C).
- The Matrix specification and the Element/Matrix.org blogs.
- The libp2p documentation and the IPFS papers.
- The Nostr NIPs (Nostr Implementation Possibilities) repository: `github.com/nostr-protocol/nips`. The protocol's design literature is itself instructive.
- The BlueSky AT Protocol documentation.
- The Secure Scuttlebutt documentation (historical, smaller community now).
- The Hypercore Protocol / Pears documentation. Successor to the Dat ecosystem.

## 12. Privacy Coins and ZK

- The CryptoNote v2 white paper (van Saberhagen, 2013). Foundation for Monero.
- The Zcash documentation and design papers.
- The Bulletproofs paper (Bünz et al., 2018).
- The Halo / Halo2 papers and documentation.
- The PLONK and Groth16 papers.
- The Privacy and Scaling Explorations (PSE) program at the Ethereum Foundation. Their writeups are technical and largely free of token-marketing framing.
- The Aztec, Penumbra, Mina documentation.
- The Tornado Cash sanctions case (US Treasury OFAC, 2022) and the Alexey Pertsev case in the Netherlands. Important context for builder-liability discussions in the Crypto-Positive subtype.

## 13. Surveillance Studies and Policy

- *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (Shoshana Zuboff, 2019). Widely cited even by those who criticize the book's analytic framing.
- Edward Snowden's body of public statements and interviews; the Snowden archive documents.
- The EFF's policy work, especially on encryption export controls, "going dark" debates, EU Chat Control, and the UK Online Safety Act.
- Meredith Whittaker's writing and talks on AI surveillance.
- The Citizen Lab reports on commercial spyware (NSO Group / Pegasus, etc.).
- Bruce Schneier, *Data and Goliath* (2015). Surveillance economy primer.

## 14. Hacker Culture and OSS Philosophy

- Eric S. Raymond, *The Cathedral and the Bazaar* (1999). Cultural foundation; the cohort has complex feelings about Raymond personally.
- Richard Stallman's *Free Software, Free Society*. Recognized as foundational; the cohort has complex feelings about Stallman personally.
- Steven Levy, *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution* (1984). Pre-cypherpunk hacker culture.
- *Coders at Work* (Peter Seibel, 2009). Interviews with senior practitioners; gives a sense of the engineering culture this cohort emerges from.
- Karl Fogel, *Producing Open Source Software* (2005, regularly updated). Operational manual for OSS projects.

## 15. Code-Is-Law and Adjacent

- Lawrence Lessig, *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace* (1999) and *Code v2* (2006). Origin of "code is law" as a concept; the cohort understands the term differently from Lessig but still cites him.
- *Bernstein v. United States*. Case establishing that source code is speech under the First Amendment.
- John Perry Barlow, *A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace* (1996). Now read with historical irony but still part of the canon.

## 17. Books for Further Reading on the Cohort's Worldview

- Cory Doctorow, various books — especially *How to Destroy Surveillance Capitalism* (2020), *Chokepoint Capitalism* (2022, with Rebecca Giblin), and the *Little Brother* / *Homeland* / *Attack Surface* trilogy.
- Vernor Vinge, *True Names* (1981). Pre-internet cypherpunk fiction; widely cited by the original list members.
- Neal Stephenson, *Cryptonomicon* (1999). Cultural touchstone; the digital-cash and crypto-history fiction many in the cohort read in their twenties.
- Bruce Sterling, *The Hacker Crackdown* (1992). Background for the hacker-political subculture.
- Glenn Greenwald, *No Place to Hide* (2014). Snowden-era reporting.
- Andy Greenberg, *Tracers in the Dark* (2022). Blockchain-tracing and the limits of Bitcoin privacy.

## 18. A Note on Source Reliability

This dossier mixes:

- **Primary documents** (the manifesto, the design papers, the protocol specs). High confidence.
- **Secondary academic work** (the OSS contribution research, the LINDDUN methodology, the local-first essay). High confidence.
- **Community writings and posts** (Hacker News, Mastodon, blogs). Used for tonal and cultural signal; reliability varies; cited for texture, not as ground truth on individual cases.
- **The dossier author's interpretation** of how this material applies to the cohort's behavior, especially in the simulation cards. Explicitly interpretive.

The downstream agent should treat the dossier as evidence-informed but not infallible. Where the agent's simulation produces a confident prediction, the underlying evidence in this bibliography should be reachable. Where it doesn't, the simulation should fall back to uncertainty rather than fabricate.
